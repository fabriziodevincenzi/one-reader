# From the Sender

## Editorial project for One Reader

## 1. Purpose

From the Sender is the editorial publication behind One Reader.

It is a publication about intimate writing, correspondence, attention and the quieter possibilities of the internet. It explores what changes when a person writes to one real reader instead of speaking to an audience, a feed or an algorithm.

From the Sender exists to do three things:

1. make the ideas behind One Reader clear;
2. give the service a distinctive cultural and editorial voice;
3. help people discover, understand and trust One Reader before they use it.

It is not a product blog and it is not a general writing blog. It is an independent-feeling publication with a clear relationship to the service it supports.

## 2. Central thesis

> Writing changes when there is one real person on the other side.

One Reader is built around a simple relationship: one person writes to one person they do not know. From the Sender examines the human, cultural and practical meaning of that relationship.

The publication should return repeatedly to a few connected ideas:

- writing becomes more precise when it has a real reader;
- privacy can make communication more honest;
- anonymity can remove performance and expectation;
- a letter creates space around attention;
- communication does not need to be immediate to be meaningful;
- the internet can connect people without turning every interaction into a public performance.

## 3. Editorial position

From the Sender believes that the internet has made communication abundant but not necessarily more attentive. Many digital interactions are public, persistent, accelerated and shaped by metrics. One Reader offers a different model: private, direct, asynchronous correspondence between two people.

The publication should not argue that older forms of communication were perfect or that technology is inherently harmful. It should ask better questions about the conditions under which people can speak, listen and be present to one another.

Its perspective is pro-technology, but against unnecessary acceleration, surveillance and performance.

## 4. Editorial areas

### The sender

Writing as an intimate act: voice, hesitation, clarity, detail, sincerity and the decision to send something outward.

Possible subjects:

- writing to someone who knows nothing about you;
- the difference between an honest sentence and a beautiful sentence;
- why small details matter;
- what we leave out when we write;
- writing without performing;
- the sentence we almost deleted;
- how addressing one person changes a piece of writing.

### The reader

Attention, interpretation and the experience of being read by another person.

Possible subjects:

- what it means to read carefully;
- the value of being heard without being known;
- reading without reacting immediately;
- the difference between an audience and a reader;
- what a reader owes a writer, and what they do not;
- the dignity of an unanswered letter.

### The letter

The history, form and emotional structure of correspondence.

Possible subjects:

- letters as a form with boundaries;
- waiting, silence and reply;
- correspondence between strangers;
- the relationship between distance and intimacy;
- letters in literature and everyday life;
- what email can still be used for;
- why a letter is not simply a slower message.

### The quiet internet

The wider culture around privacy, slow communication and humane digital services.

Possible subjects:

- communication without feeds;
- asynchronous design;
- privacy as a social condition, not only a technical feature;
- independent and sustainable online services;
- technology that does not demand constant attention;
- alternatives to public metrics and visibility;
- the difference between connection and availability.

### One Reader

Transparent, practical and reflective pieces about the service itself.

Possible subjects:

- why One Reader exists;
- how private aliases work;
- why there are no public profiles;
- how matching works;
- what anonymity means in practice;
- why there are no read receipts;
- how moderation and safety are approached;
- why the service uses email;
- how membership supports the service;
- decisions made while building One Reader.

## 5. Formats

### Essays

Substantial pieces built around a clear question or argument. Essays should connect a cultural or human observation to the experience of writing and reading.

### Practical notes

Short, useful pieces that answer a specific question about writing, letters, privacy or the service.

Examples:

- How to write a first letter to someone you do not know
- What to say when you do not know what to say
- Do you have to reply to a letter?
- How anonymous correspondence works
- What happens when a letter goes unanswered?

### Field notes

First-person notes from the people building One Reader. These should explain decisions, uncertainties and principles without becoming promotional updates.

### Conversations and profiles

Interviews with writers, readers, designers, researchers and people building thoughtful alternatives to the dominant internet.

### Letters and fragments

Anonymous or adapted contributions shared only with explicit consent. No private letter should be published casually, and no contribution should turn the service into a content-collection mechanism.

## 6. Audience

From the Sender is for people who:

- miss slower and more deliberate forms of communication;
- want to write but do not necessarily consider themselves writers;
- feel tired of feeds, notifications and constant visibility;
- care about privacy and independent technology;
- are curious about strangers, weak ties and unexpected human contact;
- enjoy essays, books and ideas about language and attention.

The primary reader is not an established author. It is an ordinary person with something to say who may be looking for a quieter way to say it.

## 7. Voice and style

The voice should be:

- intelligent but accessible;
- intimate but not sentimental;
- reflective but grounded;
- precise without sounding academic;
- literary without becoming ornamental;
- confident without pretending to have final answers;
- practical when explaining the service;
- curious about people and skeptical of unnecessary systems.

Each piece should begin with a real question, observation or tension. It should develop one central idea clearly and end with a thought that remains open rather than a forced conclusion.

Avoid:

- generic productivity advice;
- empty claims about mindfulness or connection;
- exaggerated promises about loneliness or mental health;
- product language disguised as editorial writing;
- treating every letter as the beginning of a friendship;
- romanticising the past or condemning all modern technology;
- publishing private experiences without clear consent.

## 8. Relationship with One Reader

From the Sender should explain why One Reader exists, but it should not repeat the product pitch in every article.

The publication earns attention through ideas. One Reader is introduced as the practical expression of those ideas.

Every article may lead to one of three next steps:

- understand the service;
- explore the idea further;
- write a first letter.

Calls to action should remain quiet and specific:

- See how One Reader works
- Read about privacy
- Start your first letter
- Write to someone

## 9. Editorial standards

Every article should have:

- a clear central question;
- a distinct reason to exist;
- a strong title and useful summary;
- an identifiable audience need or curiosity;
- accurate research where research is used;
- a natural connection to the publication’s central thesis;
- one appropriate next step for the reader.

Research should support the argument, not replace it. First-person writing is welcome, especially when explaining the design and philosophy of One Reader, but the publication should gradually include outside voices and lived experience.

## 10. Initial editorial programme

The first phase should establish the publication rather than attempt to cover everything.

Recommended starting set:

1. Why One Reader exists
2. Writing to one person
3. The reader in your head
4. How to write a first letter to a stranger
5. Why One Reader uses email
6. What privacy means here
7. The difference between a beautiful sentence and an honest one
8. What happens when a letter goes unanswered?
9. Leaving the feed without leaving the world
10. A conversation with someone who thinks carefully about writing, privacy or attention

The first editorial package could be presented as:

> **From the Sender: Issue 01 — Writing to One Person**

## 11. Long-term direction

From the Sender can grow from a Journal into a small editorial world around One Reader: essays, interviews, practical notes, a newsletter and occasional themed issues.

It should remain smaller and more focused than a general-interest magazine. Its strength should come from coherence, quality and a recognisable point of view.

The long-term ambition is not to publish more. It is to publish pieces that make the reader see writing, reading and online connection slightly differently — and make One Reader feel like the natural place to act on that feeling.
