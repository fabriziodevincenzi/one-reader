Email is one of the few pieces of internet infrastructure whose basic promise is still recognisable after more than fifty years: one person can address another person across systems they do not control.

## A protocol nobody owns

In 1971, Ray Tomlinson adapted an existing ARPANET program so that mail could travel between different computers. He used `@` to separate a person from the machine receiving the message. The symbol is still present in every ordinary email address. [The Internet Society’s history of the internet](https://www.internetsociety.org/internet/history-internet/brief-history-internet/) describes Tomlinson’s work as one of the early applications that made networked communication useful to people, not only to machines.

The machinery around email has changed repeatedly. SMTP, the protocol used to transfer mail between servers, was formalised by Jon Postel in [RFC 821 in 1982](https://www.rfc-editor.org/info/rfc821/). Later standards added richer formats, authentication, encryption and filtering. What has endured is not an unchanged technical object, but an open arrangement: different providers can exchange messages because they agree to speak a common language.

Compare that with most of the ways we talk today. WhatsApp, Instagram DMs and Slack are gardens with a landlord. Their rules, identities and doors belong to one company. Email is closer to a shared road: providers can build different vehicles, but no single one owns the destination itself.

## Designed for trust, then forced to learn suspicion

Early network mail assumed a small community of trusted researchers. It was not designed for spam, phishing or billions of daily messages. That history explains both email’s weaknesses and its resilience. The system had to acquire new layers of protection without losing its central ability to connect unlike institutions.

Postel’s famous engineering maxim — be conservative in what you send, liberal in what you accept — was advice for imperfect machines. It is not a complete philosophy for human communication, but it contains a useful reminder: a shared language survives by making room for difference.

The inbox has since become crowded with marketing, notifications and automated urgency. [The last personal email you received](/journal/the-last-personal-email-you-received/) considers what happens when a private form is asked to carry too much commercial noise. Yet the underlying possibility remains unusually simple: an address can outlast the service that first gave it to you.

## Nothing new, on purpose

One Reader does not need to invent a new technical language. It uses the old one to protect a narrower purpose. No feed decides who is visible, no publisher stands between writer and reader, and no platform needs to turn correspondence into a public performance.

Fifty-four years after Tomlinson’s experiment, the most radical thing about email may be its lack of a single owner. One Reader tries to make that openness feel personal again: one letter, one reader, enough time for the words to arrive.
