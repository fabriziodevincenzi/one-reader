Loneliness is not determined only by how much communication we receive. It also depends on the quality of the presence on the other side.

A system designed to answer patiently can still leave us outside a real relationship. A response may be useful without coming from someone who chose to be there. In a randomized University of British Columbia study of 296 first-year students, only those who exchanged daily messages with a randomly paired peer reported less loneliness; a supportive chatbot performed no better than journaling. [The university’s summary](https://news.ubc.ca/2026/04/texting-with-a-stranger-beats-a-chatbot-at-easing-loneliness/) makes the distinction clear.

This is not an argument that AI is useless. It is an argument that artificial availability is not the same as human reciprocity. A stranger may misunderstand, take time, or choose not to answer. That uncertainty is not merely a flaw. It is evidence that another person is making a choice.

One Reader begins there: one person writes to one person they do not know. No perfect compatibility, no audience, no promise that every letter becomes a lasting friendship. Just the possibility that a real human being will decide to be present for the length of a message.

A chatbot can always be available. A stranger can choose to be there. The second kind of presence is more fragile, and for that reason it can mean more.
