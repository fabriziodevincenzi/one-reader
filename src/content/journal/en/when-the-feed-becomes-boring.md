People do not leave social media only because there is too much content. They also leave because too much of the content begins to look and sound the same.

That distinction matters. A feed can be full without feeling alive. It can offer an endless supply of posts while giving the reader very little sense of discovery. When every item arrives in a familiar format, from a familiar kind of account, carrying a familiar emotional cue, abundance starts to behave like repetition.

A 2026 study in *BMC Psychology* describes this as information similarity overload: users can withdraw from social communities when the information they receive becomes excessively similar. The problem is not simply quantity. It is the experience of encountering many variations of the same thing, until attention no longer expects to be surprised. [The study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40359-026-04384-x) connects this overload to withdrawal behaviour on social-networking sites.

This helps explain a common confusion. Someone may still want contact, conversation and culture while no longer wanting the feed that supposedly provides them. Leaving the feed is not necessarily a rejection of other people. It may be a rejection of an environment where the difference between a friend’s thought, a creator’s performance and a brand’s campaign has been flattened into one continuous stream.

The feed is designed to remove the pause between things. It learns what keeps attention and supplies more of it. Over time, relevance can become a kind of enclosure: the system knows what we tend to click, so it gives us fewer reasons to encounter anything outside that expectation. The result is not silence but saturation without plurality.

That is why a quieter online space can feel more social than a crowded one. A letter has a sender with a particular reason for writing. A reply has a person who had to decide to make time. The exchange is narrower than a feed, but its narrowness protects difference. See also [Leaving the feed without leaving the world](/journal/leaving-the-feed-without-leaving-the-world/), which asks what disconnection can preserve.

One Reader takes the opposite approach to the infinite stream. It does not promise more things to consume. It creates a limited situation in which one person writes to one person, and the value of the exchange depends on the attention inside it rather than on the volume around it.

The question is not whether we can tolerate another feed. It is whether we still want a digital life made almost entirely of things selected because they resemble what already held us.
