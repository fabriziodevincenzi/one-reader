A large network can make a person more connected in public while leaving them unsupported in private. The number of contacts we accumulate online tells us very little about whether those contacts can recognise, understand or accompany us.

A 2026 study in *Public Health Reports* examined this question among 1,512 adults aged 30 to 70 in the United States. Participants with the highest share of social-media contacts they had never met in person had more than twice the odds of reporting loneliness compared with those in the lowest quartile. The presence of more close personal friends on social media was not significantly associated with lower loneliness. [The study](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00333549261443288) is cross-sectional, so it cannot establish causality. But it challenges the assumption that a bigger social graph is automatically a stronger social world.

The result is not that online friends are unreal. People can form genuine relationships with someone they have never met in person. It shows instead that contact without sufficient closeness may correlate with loneliness, while the label of close friend on a platform does not guarantee protection from it.

Platforms count followers, friends, likes and replies because those things can be quantified. They cannot easily count memory, patience, attention, mutual knowledge or the confidence that one’s absence would be noticed.

Social media encourages us to confuse reach with belonging. A person can be surrounded by names and still have nowhere to place a difficult thought. They can receive constant reactions without being known by anyone who has the context to understand what the reaction means.

One Reader begins with a deliberately smaller unit: one person and one other person. The point is not that every exchange becomes intimate, or that a stranger is automatically more meaningful than a friend. Connection needs a form capable of holding attention. [A letter is a small room](/journal/a-letter-is-a-small-room/) describes why limitation can make a relationship more legible.

A useful social space should not ask how many people are available to us. It should ask whether there is somewhere a person can arrive without having to compete with everyone else for recognition.

A network is large by counting. A relationship becomes large by making room.

Source: [Closeness of Social Media Contacts and Loneliness Among US Adults](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00333549261443288).
