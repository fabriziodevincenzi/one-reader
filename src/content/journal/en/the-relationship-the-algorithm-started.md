Many online relationships begin before we choose them. They begin with exposure: an account appears repeatedly, its language becomes familiar, and familiarity slowly takes the shape of connection.

A 2026 study in *Information Processing & Management* models this process through a panel of 1,015 creators, 794,051 account-days and 829,783 posts. Its central finding is straightforward: exposure allocated by a platform produces follower inflow, which is then balanced by churn. What looks like organic audience growth is partly the result of a distribution system deciding who gets seen.

This does not make every follower relationship false. It shows that social platforms often confuse repeated exposure with personal discovery. The person we follow may feel familiar because we have encountered them many times, but the initial meeting was arranged by a ranking system.

The platform’s incentives are not the same as ours. Platforms want attention to circulate toward accounts that can hold large audiences. Creators want reach, growth and conversion. Users may simply want to encounter something meaningful. These goals overlap occasionally, but they are not identical.

The feed turns visibility into a social currency. The more visible an account becomes, the more likely it is to be seen again; the more often it is seen, the more natural it feels to follow. A small number of accounts become companions in the loose sense that they accompany our days, even though they do not know we are there.

Private correspondence begins elsewhere. It does not require a person to become visible to thousands before becoming worth answering. It asks for a decision between two people, each with limited time and incomplete knowledge of the other.

One Reader is built around that smaller threshold. A stranger is not discovered because an algorithm has optimized their visibility. They become present because someone has chosen to write, and because someone else has chosen to read.

A relationship does not become more real because it reaches more people. Sometimes it becomes more real when fewer people are watching.
