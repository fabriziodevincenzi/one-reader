Writing becomes a different practice when it is addressed to one real reader. It is no longer only a private record of thought, and it is not yet a public performance. It becomes a form of social attention.

Research on first-year university students describes the difficulty of writing inside what the authors call a “horizontal attention ecosystem”: a digital environment in which assignments, messages, platforms and media all seem equally entitled to interrupt us. Students try to protect their writing through restrictions and deliberate arrangements, but they often continue to experience writing as a solitary act. [The study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S875546152500009X) argues that writing should also be understood as embodied and social.

Between the diary and the post lies another possibility: writing that is intimate without being entirely private, social without being public.

A letter occupies that middle space. It asks the writer to make an experience intelligible to someone who does not already know the background. The reader cannot fill every gap with shared memory. The sentence has to carry more of its own meaning.

That demand can improve writing, not because it makes the writer more polished, but because it makes the writer more responsible. A vague feeling has to become a thought. A reaction has to become an account. An impulse has to become something another person can receive without being required to guess what was meant.

The single reader also changes the pressure of publication. There is no need to anticipate every possible interpretation or construct a version of the self that can survive a large audience. The writer can remain partial. They can say one true thing without turning it into a position.

One Reader offers this different scale. A person writes to one reader they do not know, and the writing becomes social through its address rather than through its reach. [The part of reading that cannot be automated](/journal/the-part-of-reading-that-cannot-be-automated/) begins from a related idea: attention can be valuable precisely because it is not optimised for visibility.

Writing for someone is not a lesser form of writing for everyone. It may be the place where language becomes most accountable, because there is a person on the other side—but not a crowd waiting to judge the result.

Source: [All the attention, all the time: How first-year students experience writing in a horizontal digital ecosystem](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S875546152500009X).
