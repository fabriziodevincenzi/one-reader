In June 2026 [Incogni](https://blog.incogni.com/digital-fatigue-and-burnout/) asked a representative sample of American adults how their relationship with feeds had changed. Fifty-five percent post less than five years ago. Fifty-three percent are more selective about who can see their posts. Forty-seven percent deleted a social or messaging app because it generated stress or anxiety. Fifty-one percent say maintaining an online presence "feels like a job." Among Gen Z that figure rises to 60%. This is not a noisy exodus. It is a selective withdrawal: people stay signed up, scroll, comment less, show less.

The same survey records a double emotion after disconnection. Peace is the first answer (27%). Right behind it, more than one in five feels anxiety. [Talker Research](https://talkerresearch.com/survey-reveals-intentional-digital-disconnection-growing-among-americans/), in early 2026, on two thousand Americans, found that 50% had chosen to spend less time on screens "for their own wellbeing," led by Gen Z and millennials (63% and 57%). Eighty-four percent had introduced at least one analogue gesture into the day. Seventy percent of time spent online, for respondents, produced disconnection and loneliness more than relationship. [Cybersmile](https://www.cybersmile.org/resource/digital-wellbeing-report-2026/), the same year, on a thousand adults: 66% believe social media has damaged their mental health; 49% feel better when they use it less.

The data does not say that "social media is ending." It says that performance is ending. The feed continues to function as distribution infrastructure. What has broken is the implicit contract under which public existence and presence coincide. Those who withdraw are not necessarily going somewhere. Often they are only stopping production.

This is the point at which social fatigue is mistaken for a programme. It is not one. Deleting an app is a negative act: it removes a channel. It does not, by itself, create another recipient. The marketer who spends twelve hours a day keeping other people's accounts alive — [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/the-hidden-burnout-crisis-facing-social-media-marketers-283216), June 2026 — and the private person who posts less in order not to be exposed are doing two different jobs on the same medium. Both discover that the feed is not a place where one stays. It is a place where one performs. When performance costs more than it returns, people leave. The open question is where they enter.

Here the phenomenon connects to another already observed: mail that stops being read because an agent reads it, and the letter that starts being written again because someone, somewhere, opens it by hand. The two movements are not opposites. They are two exits from the same environment — the one in which every message is also content, every address is also an audience, every silence is also an absence to justify. [When mail stops being read](/journal/when-mail-stops-being-read/) and [The social user who no longer participates](/journal/the-social-user-who-no-longer-participates/) describe two faces of that exit.

Industry already has a ready answer: more wellbeing tools, timers, focus modes, "digital sunset." These are product answers to a protocol problem. The feed's protocol is built to maximise time spent, not the quality of the encounter. You can spend less time on an app and still have no one to write to. You can also, as Gallup shows in the 2026 World Happiness Report, use the internet in an environment saturated with social media and see trust and wellbeing worsen, especially among the youngest in Western Europe. The effect is not "the screen." It is the density of performative comparison.

For anyone still publishing for a single reader — and not for an audience to retain — this fatigue is demand data. It does not ask for another viral format. It asks for a channel in which absence is not guilt and presence is not a shift of work. A newsletter without tracking, a static site, a letter, a text that arrives when someone decides to open it: these do not solve statistical loneliness. They change the measure. The value is not how many minutes are taken from the feed. It is whether, once taken, there is still someone to whom a sentence can be addressed.

The threshold, from here on, is not "online or offline." It is "public or addressed." As long as those are confused, leaving the feed will remain a temporary relief and not a conversation.
